    01 — Overlap checker

    Two names, one receptor.

    The most common mistake online is stacking substances that act on the same target. Pick two groups and see whether they collide — with the reason, not just a red light.

    No shared target foundThese act on different targets, so there is no duplication between them. That is not the same as being safe together: interactions outside a shared receptor are a question for a doctor, not for a chart.

    Do not combine: duplicated mechanism All act on GLP-1 receptors, some also on GIP or glucagon. The maker of tirzepatide states there are no clinical data on combining it with another GLP-1 agonist. The result may be stronger side effects with no added benefit: nausea, vomiting, dehydration and hypoglycemia with other drugs. 16 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: duplicated mechanism Both are amylin analogues acting on the same receptors. No data on combining; stronger effects include nausea and hypoglycemia with insulin. 4 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same hormonal axis All stimulate or replace the growth hormone or IGF-1 axis. Effects and side effects can add up (blood glucose, fluid retention, swelling). There are no safety studies of such combinations, and some substances are not approved. 16 entries in this group.

    Do not combine on your own GnRH agonists and antagonists are different strategies for the same hormonal axis, chosen by a specialist. Mixing them without a protocol disrupts hormones. 11 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: duplicated mechanism All bind somatostatin receptors. Doubling up increases effects on blood glucose, digestion and the heart. The drug is chosen by an endocrinologist. 4 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same receptor Both stimulate the PTH1 receptor. There is no evidence of benefit from using them together, and the labels do not provide for it. 2 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: duplicated receptors All activate melanocortin receptors. PT-141 (bremelanotide) is a derivative of Melanotan II without the C-terminal amide, also described as its likely metabolite, so they act similarly. Their status differs: PT-141 is approved, Melanotan II is not. Risks: raised blood pressure, nausea, mole changes. 4 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: duplicated vasopressin receptors All act on vasopressin receptors. Desmopressin carries a risk of low sodium (hyponatremia), and doubling up increases it. These are hospital or specialist drugs. 5 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same mechanism Both stimulate the same receptor in the gut. Doubling up worsens diarrhea and dehydration risk. 2 entries in this group.

    Bleeding risk Both prevent clotting. Combining with other anticoagulants increases bleeding risk. Decisions are made by the cardiology team. 3 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same claimed goal, no data All of them (BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, GHK-Cu and the GLOW and KLOW blends) are marketed for healing or recovery, and their mechanisms in humans are unconfirmed. Stacking several unstudied substances multiplies unknowns and makes it hard to tell what helped or harmed. 9 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same claimed goal, no data They target the same muscle growth axis. None has confirmed safety in healthy people, and ACE-031 was stopped because of vascular effects. 8 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same claimed goal, no data All are described as acting on the central nervous system. Combining them with each other or with psychiatric drugs has not been studied. 13 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same claimed goal, no data Marketed as supporting mitochondria or slowing ageing, mainly on the basis of preclinical studies. Combinations are not studied. 9 entries in this group.

    Do not combine: same claimed goal, no data Marketed for fat loss but without convincing clinical results. Combining them with each other or with approved obesity drugs has not been studied. 7 entries in this group.

    These act on different targets, so there is no duplication between them. That is not the same as being safe together: interactions outside a shared receptor are a question for a doctor, not for a chart.

    The rule

    Same receptor,
    no added benefit,
    added risk.

    Doubling a mechanism rarely doubles the benefit, but it can add to the side effects of both.

    02 — Peptide of the day

    Pramlintide

    2026-10-30

    Pramlintide is a synthetic analogue of amylin, a hormone released together with insulin by the beta cells of the pancreas. Natural human amylin clumps into deposits easily, so in pramlintide three amino acids were swapped for proline, which made it soluble. The FDA approved it in 2005 (Symlin) as a mealtime add-on to insulin in people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. It slows gastric emptying and reduces the glucagon surge after meals; nausea is a common side effect, and with insulin it raises the risk of severe hypoglycemia. It is a prescription medicine used under medical supervision.

    04 — Questions

    Are peptides proteins?

    Peptides and proteins are made of the same amino acids. Peptides are shorter, proteins longer and folded into a stable structure. The border is conventional, often around 50 amino acids.

    Do peptides in creams reach the deeper skin?

    It depends on the molecule and carrier. Large molecules cross the epidermis poorly, so makers modify peptides, for example by attaching a fatty acid. Much of the data comes from lab studies.

    Why are peptide drugs usually injected?

    Peptides are digested by gut acid and enzymes, trapped by mucus and cross the gut lining poorly. An exception is semaglutide tablets, approved in 2019, which work with an absorption enhancer.

    Are peptides bought online safe?

    “Research only” products are not controlled like drugs and human data on them are scarce. Details are in the blog article.
