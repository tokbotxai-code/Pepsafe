    Groups

    Substances that share a target.

    Stacking inside one group repeats a mechanism instead of adding a second one. Each group says what it shares and why that matters.

    Do not combine: duplicated mechanism

    GLP-1 agonists and related (GIP, glucagon)

    All act on GLP-1 receptors, some also on GIP or glucagon. The maker of tirzepatide states there are no clinical data on combining it with another GLP-1 agonist. The result may be stronger side effects with no added benefit: nausea, vomiting, dehydration and hypoglycemia with other drugs.

    Somatostatin analogues

    All bind somatostatin receptors. Doubling up increases effects on blood glucose, digestion and the heart. The drug is chosen by an endocrinologist.

    Parathyroid hormone analogues

    Both stimulate the PTH1 receptor. There is no evidence of benefit from using them together, and the labels do not provide for it.

    Do not combine: duplicated receptors

    Melanocortin receptor agonists

    All activate melanocortin receptors. PT-141 (bremelanotide) is a derivative of Melanotan II without the C-terminal amide, also described as its likely metabolite, so they act similarly. Their status differs: PT-141 is approved, Melanotan II is not. Risks: raised blood pressure, nausea, mole changes.

    Vasopressin and its analogues

    All act on vasopressin receptors. Desmopressin carries a risk of low sodium (hyponatremia), and doubling up increases it. These are hospital or specialist drugs.
