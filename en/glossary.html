    Glossary

    The words, in plain language.

    Terms that come up on every page, explained once so the rest of the site reads easily.

    "Research use only"
    A label on products sold as laboratory reagents. It means the product is not a medicine and has not passed the quality and sterility checks required for medicines.
    Adverse effect
    A harmful or unwanted effect of a medicine or substance. Trials record all such events, including ones not proven to be caused by the substance.
    Agonist
    A substance that activates a receptor the way a natural signal does.
    Amino acid
    A building block of peptides and proteins. The body uses 20 standard ones.
    Analog
    A substance similar to a natural molecule, chemically modified, often to last longer.
    Antagonist
    A substance that blocks a receptor without activating it.
    Approved drug
    A substance authorised by a regulator (e.g. FDA, EMA) for a stated use.
    Bioavailability
    The share of a substance that reaches the blood in active form. Most peptides have very low bioavailability when swallowed, because enzymes digest them.
    Certificate of analysis (COA)
    A document with test results for a product batch. Its value depends on who tested it and how; a certificate issued by the seller itself proves little.
    Clinical trial phase
    Stages of testing a medicine in people. Phase 1: safety in a small group. Phase 2: whether it works and at what amount. Phase 3: large comparative trials before approval. Phase 4: monitoring after it reaches the market.
    Compounded drug
    A medicine prepared by a pharmacy for a specific patient rather than made in a factory. In the US, compounded drugs are not reviewed by the FDA like standard medicines.
    Concentration
    Mass of substance divided by solution volume, e.g. mg/ml.
    Control group
    Study participants who do not get the tested substance (they get placebo or standard care). Without one, it is impossible to tell whether an improvement came from the substance.
    DEXA
    A low-dose X-ray scan of body composition. It measures fat, lean mass and bone; shifts in water and glycogen can exaggerate short-term lean-mass loss.
    Double-blind
    A study in which neither participants nor researchers know who gets the medicine and who gets placebo. This reduces the influence of expectations on the results.
    Dual and triple agonist
    A molecule that activates two (for example GLP-1 and GIP) or three (GLP-1, GIP and glucagon) different receptors at once. More receptors can mean a stronger effect, but also more unknowns about safety.
    Endotoxin
    A fragment of the bacterial cell wall that can cause fever and inflammation even after the bacteria are dead. Medicines are tested for endotoxins; "research" products often are not.
    GIP
    The second incretin hormone from the gut (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide). It boosts insulin release after meals. Tirzepatide acts on both GIP and GLP-1 receptors.
    GLP-1
    A gut hormone (glucagon-like peptide-1) released after meals. It raises glucose-dependent insulin release, slows stomach emptying and reduces appetite. Natural GLP-1 breaks down within minutes, so medicines use longer-lasting analogues.
    Glucagon
    A pancreatic hormone that raises blood glucose and increases energy use by the liver. Retatrutide and survodutide also act on the glucagon receptor, which is meant to raise energy expenditure.
    Growth hormone secretagogue
    A substance that prompts the pituitary gland to release growth hormone. This group includes GHRH analogues and ghrelin mimetics.
    Half-life
    Time for the blood concentration of a substance to fall by half.
    IGF-1
    Insulin-like growth factor 1, made mainly by the liver in response to growth hormone. It carries out many of growth hormone's effects; its level is used to assess the growth hormone axis.
    Incretins
    Gut hormones that increase insulin release after a meal. The main ones are GLP-1 and GIP. Incretin medicines mimic their action.
    Indication
    The disease or condition a medicine is approved for. Off-label use has not been assessed by the regulator for that purpose.
    Lean mass
    Everything in the body except fat: muscle, organs, bone and water. With fast weight loss, part of the weight lost is lean mass.
    Lyophilizate
    A substance freeze-dried under vacuum and supplied as a powder in a vial.
    Melanocortins
    A group of hormones and receptors (MC1R–MC5R) that affect skin pigmentation, appetite and sexual function, among others. Afamelanotide and bremelanotide act on them.
    Meta-analysis
    An analysis that combines the results of many studies on the same question. It gives a stronger conclusion than one study, provided the studies were of good quality.
    Off-label
    Using an approved medicine for a different condition, amount or patient group than its label states. Doctors may prescribe this way, but the evidence is often weaker.
    Overdose
    Taking more than is safe. Symptoms need urgent help.
    Peptide
    A short chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds. Usually up to about 50 residues; longer chains are proteins.
    Placebo
    An inactive substance given to a control group to separate the real effect of a medicine from expectation and chance. Studies without placebo or a control group give weak evidence.
    Preclinical study
    Research in cells or animals before a substance is tested in people. Most results at this stage are not confirmed later in humans.
    Prescribing information (label)
    The official document for a medicine approved by a regulator: uses, dosing, contraindications and side effects. In the US it is the label on DailyMed; in the EU and UK it is the SmPC.
    Purity (HPLC)
    The percentage of a sample made up of one compound, measured by chromatography. High purity does not say whether it is the right compound; that needs an identity test such as mass spectrometry.
    RCT
    Randomised controlled trial with a control group (placebo). Usually the strongest evidence of effect.
    Receptor
    A protein on or inside a cell that a specific molecule fits like a key in a lock. Binding triggers a specific response in the cell.
    Reconstitution
    Dissolving the powder in a solvent before use. It changes concentration, not the amount of substance.
    Regulators (FDA, EMA, MHRA)
    Agencies that approve medicines: the FDA in the US, the EMA in the European Union and the MHRA in the UK. Approval in one country does not mean approval in another.
    Sterility
    The absence of living microorganisms in a product. It is required for medicines given by routes that bypass the gut; without it there is a risk of infection.
    Systematic review
    A structured collection of all studies on a topic, gathered by set rules. It shows how much is known and how strong the evidence is.
    Tachyphylaxis
    A rapid loss of effect when the same substance is given repeatedly. It affects, for example, some growth-hormone-releasing peptides.
    WADA
    World Anti-Doping Agency. Publishes the list of substances banned in sport.
    WADA S0 list
    The WADA Prohibited List category for substances not approved for human use. It covers many peptides sold "for research".
