    Warnings

    What regulators said. What marketing says.

    Official warnings, the selling tricks that come up again and again, and claims that do not survive contact with the research.

    Regulators

    Tirzepatide recall from compounding pharmacy Apothecary Pharma (US), 24 Sep 2026

    Apothecary Pharma LLC of Cary, North Carolina, a compounding pharmacy (not the maker of Mounjaro or Zepbound), voluntarily recalled its tirzepatide injection 18 mg/mL and an NAD+ injection across the US. The reason in the FDA enforcement report: lack of assurance of sterility. The FDA classified the recall as Class II, meaning the product may cause temporary or medically reversible health effects, with serious problems unlikely. Compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. Anyone who has this product should contact their doctor; the lot numbers are listed in the FDA report.

    FDA warning letters to peptide sellers

    The FDA sends warning letters when it decides a firm is selling unapproved drugs. In several cases the agency stated plainly that a “for research purposes only” label changes nothing, and it treated website claims, guides, calculators and bacteriostatic water sold alongside as evidence the products were meant for people.

    Counterfeit Ozempic in the legitimate supply chain

    The FDA warned three times about counterfeit Ozempic 1 mg that reached the normal US supply: lots NAR0074 (December 2023), PAR0362 (April 2025) and PAR1229 (December 2025). In the first batch the needles were counterfeit too, so sterility could not be confirmed.

    Salt forms instead of the active ingredient

    The FDA states that “semaglutide sodium” and “semaglutide acetate” are different active ingredients from semaglutide and that it is not aware of any lawful basis for using them. It also warns about products falsely labelled “for research” or “not for human consumption” sold straight to consumers.

    What was actually in products bought online

    In a study published in JAMA Network Open, semaglutide was test-purchased from six no-prescription vendors. Three orders never arrived. In the three that did, purity was 7 to 14 percent against an advertised 99, and content was 29 to 39 percent above the label. An independent analysis of melanotan II found 4.3 to 8.8 mg of peptide in vials labelled 10 mg.

    How to check this yourself

    The FDA runs an open database of products subject to recalls and warnings. You type a name and see the date, the firm, what the violation was and what action was taken. The agency itself notes the database covers only a fraction of the products on the market, so absence from it does not mean something is safe.

    Selling tricks

    “For research purposes only”

    A formula meant to shift responsibility onto the buyer. It is not a legal category that protects a seller: the FDA judged intended use by what the firm writes and what it sells alongside.

    A certificate that certifies nothing

    A scanned document with no laboratory name, date, batch number or method. The same file gets reused across sellers and batches.

    Before-and-after photos

    Lighting, time of day, hydration and muscle tension change appearance more than most substances do. Such photos prove nothing.

    A list of twenty effects

    The longer the list of promises, the weaker the evidence. It usually comes from cell or animal studies, at doses and routes that do not translate to people.

    A study that is not about the product

    A seller links a real paper, but it covers a different substance, a different form or only cells. It is worth opening the link and reading who was studied.

    Urgency and limits

    Countdown timers, “last units”, a discount only today. A sales device, not information about quality.

    Faceless reviews

    Reviews on a seller’s site are moderated by that seller. There is no way to know who wrote them or whether anyone received the same product.

    Vocabulary that dodges the law

    “Not for human consumption”, “reference material”, “reagent”. The wording changes the description, not what is inside or who it is sold to.

    Myth or fact

    Myth

    Research peptides bought online are the same as pharmacy medicines.

    “Research only” products do not have to meet the sterility, purity and dose-accuracy standards that apply to medicines. Vial contents may differ from the label, and batches are usually not independently checked.

    Myth

    If it is a natural peptide, it is safe.

    Insulin, glucagon and vasopressin are natural too, and the wrong dose can kill. Safety depends on dose, purity, route and human studies, not on where the molecule comes from.

    Myth

    BPC-157 is well studied in humans.

    A 2025 review found only 3 small human studies, under 30 people in total, without control groups. Almost all data come from animal studies.

    Myth

    In 2026 the FDA approved BPC-157 and TB-500.

    In July 2026 an FDA advisory committee only recommended adding them to the list of compounding ingredients (8 to 6). That is a non-binding recommendation, not drug approval, and it does not cover products from online shops.

    Myth

    Two GLP-1 drugs at once means faster weight loss.

    They act on the same receptor, so mainly side effects add up: nausea, vomiting, dehydration. The maker of tirzepatide states there are no data on combining it with other GLP-1 agonists.

    Myth

    After stopping an obesity drug the weight stays off.

    In withdrawal trials (STEP 4 for semaglutide, SURMOUNT-4 for tirzepatide) a large part of the lost weight returned within a year. These drugs work while they are taken.

    Partly true

    A peptide pill works just like an injection.

    Most peptides are digested in the stomach and gut. Exceptions such as oral semaglutide use a special absorption enhancer, need much higher doses and must be taken on an empty stomach.

    Partly true

    Collagen powder rebuilds your skin.

    A 2023 meta-analysis of 26 trials found a modest improvement in skin elasticity and hydration. Trials were small, some results subjective, and it does not replace sun protection.

    Partly true

    A certificate of analysis (CoA) guarantees quality.

    A CoA shows the result for one sample. If the seller issued it, without an independent lab and a batch number matching the package, it proves little. It does not replace sterility testing or drug-grade control.

    Myth

    Peptide cream works like Botox.

    Botulinum toxin is injected and blocks nerves in the muscle. Peptides in creams barely cross the epidermis, and evidence comes mostly from lab work and small manufacturer studies.

    Worth checking

    GLP-1 drugs are approved only for diabetes.

    Not true the other way round: several (Wegovy, Zepbound, Saxenda, and since 2026 the Foundayo pill) are separately approved for obesity, and Wegovy also for cardiovascular risk reduction. They are still prescription drugs under medical supervision.
